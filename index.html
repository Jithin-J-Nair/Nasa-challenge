Nothing on the Moon rusts. Nothing on the Moon blows away.

On 20 July 1969, NASA's Apollo 11 left Eagle's descent stage at Tranquility Base. It is a starting point for this archive: an engineered object, deliberately left in place after people used it to reach another world.

The Moon has no atmosphere, rain, or biology to weather surface hardware in the way Earth does. NASA's Catalogue of Manmade Material on the Moon records Apollo equipment, experiments, and other U.S. material item by item. This site uses that catalogue as a source, rather than presenting an unsupported total mass.

Mars is different: dust, wind, large temperature swings, and landing risk all shape what remains. NASA missions such as Viking, Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, Perseverance, and InSight make up the centre of this Martian record. Contextual hardware from other agencies is available only through the Other agencies filter.

Beyond the planets, NASA's Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 continue to return measurements from interstellar space. Their current distances are presented here as dated approximations from NASA's Voyager status page, not as extrapolated live trajectories.

My battery is low and it's getting dark. A poet's translation of Opportunity's final telemetry · 10 June 2018 · by Jacob Margolis

This archive exists because most people only ever hear about the missions that are still working. But the machines that stopped are the ones with the stories. They were built by people, launched on fire, and abandoned on purpose — and every one of them bought us something we did not have before.

Derelict ≠ useless

Apollo 15's laser mirrors still bounce photons from Earth-based telescopes. The experiment never ended — the crew just went home.

Failure is data

NASA reports that the Soviet Mars 3 lander stopped transmitting after 14.5 seconds. It remains a contextual record; NASA mission records stay first in this archive.

Leaving things is a choice

Cassini and Galileo were deliberately destroyed inside their planets so that ocean worlds like Enceladus and Europa stay uncontaminated.

On board both Voyagers

A phonograph with no needle, thrown into an ocean with no shore.

Each Voyager carries a 30-centimetre gold-plated copper phonograph record, sealed in an aluminium jacket with a uranium-238 clock so a finder can date it. The cover is etched with instructions: how to build a needle, how fast to spin it, and — because the builders assumed whoever found it would not know where we are — a map.

Fourteen lines radiate from a point. They are the periods of fourteen pulsars, written in binary, in units of the hydrogen spin-flip transition. Any civilisation that knows pulsars can triangulate the Sun. Below it, the hyperfine transition of hydrogen gives the unit of length and time for everything else.

What they taught us

The hardware is silent. The knowledge it bought is not. Each of these facts exists because a machine went somewhere and did not come back.

Discovery 01 · Moon
Surface experiments kept teaching after the crews left.
Apollo surface experimentsNASA lunar research record Apollo instruments returned measurements from the Moon after the crews departed. This archive does not characterize local surface disturbance as a measured planetary climate change claim.
Discovery 02 · Mars
Mars was once the most Earth-like place in the solar system.
Opportunity, Spirit, Curiosity, Perseverance2004 – present Hematite concretions that formed in groundwater. Ninety-percent-pure silica from a hot spring. Mudstone laid down in a neutral-pH freshwater lake with every element life needs. All of it read by wheels.
Discovery 03 · Saturn
An ocean world is spraying itself into space.
Cassini at Enceladus2005 – 2017 Cassini flew through geysers erupting from Enceladus' south pole and tasted them: water vapour, salts, silica grains consistent with hydrothermal vents, and simple organics. A moon smaller than Arizona has a global ocean, heat, chemistry and water.
Discovery 04 · Deep space
The Sun's bubble has an edge, and we crossed it.
Voyager 1 & 22012 & 2018 Both spacecraft watched galactic cosmic rays surge and solar particles collapse as they passed through the heliopause — the boundary where the solar wind gives way to interstellar gas. We now have measurements from outside.
